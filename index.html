Before we begin — a question for you.

Before we teach you anything about painting, we want to ask you one thing. Take a moment. Be honest with yourself.

🏗️ This is a construction trade course. It covers professional painting of homes, commercial buildings, and structures — surface prep, primers, coatings, tools, safety, and how to get hired. It is not an art course, a decorating course, or a DIY course.

Before we teach you anything, we want to ask you one thing. Take a moment. Be honest with yourself.

💭 What do you think a professional construction painter actually does all day?

Write it down, say it out loud, or just think about it. By the time you finish this course, your answer will be completely different — and that's exactly the point.

What this course is — and what it isn't

This course will give you the knowledge and know-how to become employable. It will teach you what every surface, tool, product, and technique is — and why it matters. It will prepare you to walk onto a job site, speak the language, follow direction, and start learning from day one.

What it won't do is replace practice. No course can. The real skills — the clean cut line, the smooth roll, the perfect prep — those come from repetition on the job. Every painter you'll ever admire got good the same way: they showed up, they paid attention, and they practiced. Over and over.

Your goal right now is simple: become employable. Get hired. Get on a job site. Everything else follows from there.

So what is this trade, really?

Most people picture a painter as someone rolling white paint on a bedroom wall. That's like saying a chef is someone who boils pasta. It's not wrong — it's just a tiny sliver of a much bigger world.

Professional painters are the people who transform spaces. Before a building is handed over, before a family moves in, before a hotel reopens — painters are the last trade to touch every surface. They protect buildings from the elements, restore surfaces that would otherwise deteriorate, and turn raw structures into finished, livable spaces.

They work on homes, commercial buildings, schools, hospitals, bridges, industrial facilities, and everything in between. They apply paint, stain, varnish, lacquer, epoxy coatings, and waterproofing systems. They read blueprints, calculate materials, manage crews, and build businesses.

"Most beginners think: 'I need to learn how to paint.' Pros know: 'I need to learn prep, consistency, and efficiency.' That's the difference between a $20/hour painter and a $300–$1,000/day contractor."

Chapter 1 · The Trade

The world you're about to enter.

Before you hold a brush professionally, you need to understand what you're walking into — the career arc, the earning potential, and what employers are actually looking for on day one. Knowledge gets you hired. Practice makes you great.

The numbers don't lie

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics projects approximately 16,500 new painter job openings every year through 2032. The median annual wage for painters in 2024 was around $48,990, with top earners in commercial and industrial work exceeding $80,000–$100,000. Painting contractors who build their own businesses regularly earn $150,000–$300,000 per year.

You don't need a four-year degree. No student debt. No years in a classroom before you earn your first dollar. The painting trade is one of the few remaining careers in America where you can start earning real money within weeks of your first day of study.

Chapter 2 · Safety

Safety isn't a rule. It's a culture.

Every experienced painter will tell you the same thing: the most important skill in this trade isn't knowing how to roll a wall. It's knowing how to come home safe every day.

Why safety comes first

Painters work at heights, handle chemical products, and operate in tight or poorly ventilated spaces. Falls are the number one cause of construction fatalities in the United States. Paint fumes, solvents, and lead-based paint (found in older buildings) pose serious long-term health risks. None of these dangers are inevitable — they are all manageable with the right knowledge and habits.

The four rules that protect you every day

Rule 1 — Ladder and scaffold safety

  • Inspect every ladder before use — check for cracks, loose rungs, and damaged feet
  • Set extension ladders at a 4:1 ratio (1 foot out for every 4 feet up)
  • Never overreach — your belt buckle should stay between the side rails
  • Three points of contact at all times: two hands, one foot or two feet, one hand
  • On scaffolding, always use guardrails and ensure planks are fully secured

Rule 2 — Chemical and fume safety

  • Always read the Safety Data Sheet (SDS) for every product you use
  • Oil-based paints, lacquers, and solvents require strong ventilation — open windows, use fans
  • Use a respirator (not just a dust mask) when spraying or working with oil-based products
  • Never mix unknown chemicals — some combinations produce toxic gases
  • Store solvents away from heat sources — they are highly flammable

Rule 3 — Lead paint awareness

  • Any building built before 1978 may contain lead-based paint
  • Sanding or scraping lead paint releases hazardous dust — use wet methods and HEPA respirators
  • EPA's Renovation, Repair and Painting (RRP) Rule requires certification before disturbing lead paint in pre-1978 residential buildings
  • If in doubt, test before you sand — lead test kits cost under $15 at any hardware store

Rule 4 — Use your PPE every time

  • Safety glasses — always when sanding, scraping, or painting overhead
  • Gloves — protect hands from solvents and chemicals
  • Respirator — required with sprayers, oil-based products, and in enclosed spaces
  • Non-slip footwear — essential on ladders and scaffolding
  • Drop cloths — protect floors from slipping hazards as well as drips
⚠️ The most dangerous words in the trade: "It's just latex — it'll be fine." Even water-based paints can irritate airways in enclosed spaces. Always ventilate. Always read the label. The painters who get hurt are the ones who skipped the safety step because they'd done it a hundred times before.

Chapter 4 · Tools

The tools of the trade.

A professional painter is only as good as their tools — and knowing every tool by name and function before your first day immediately marks you as serious.

🛒 What to buy first: A quality 2" angled brush (~$15), a 9" roller frame + 3/8" nap cover (~$20), a roller tray, and a 5-in-1 tool (~$12). These four items cover 80% of what a helper needs and cost under $60 total.

Brushes — your most precise tool

🖌️
2" Angled Sash Brush
Your workhorse for cutting in at edges, trim, and corners. The angled tip gives you control where rollers can't reach.
🖌️
3–4" Wide Brush
For painting large flat areas by hand, wide trim, and doors. Covers ground fast.
🖌️
1" Trim Brush
Detail work in tight spaces — window muntins, narrow trim, and touch-ups.
ℹ️ Synthetic vs. Natural bristles: Use synthetic (nylon/polyester) brushes for latex (water-based) paints — they hold their shape. Use natural bristle brushes for oil-based paints and stains. Never use a natural bristle brush with latex — it will go limp and ruin your work.

Chapter 5 · Surface Preparation

Where pros separate themselves.

"Bad prep = peeling paint, callbacks, angry clients. Surface preparation is 70% of the job."

Here is the single most important lesson in this entire course: the quality of a paint job is determined before a drop of paint is ever applied. Professional painters obsess over prep. Beginners rush past it. That difference is visible in the finished work — and it's the difference between a one-time job and a referral business.

Cleaning surfaces

  • Remove all dirt, grease, and grime — paint will not adhere to contaminated surfaces
  • Use TSP (trisodium phosphate) or a TSP substitute for kitchen walls, high-grease areas
  • Use mildicide cleaner on any surface with mold or mildew — never paint over mold
  • Allow surfaces to dry completely before any further prep or painting
  • On exterior surfaces, pressure washing is standard — but control the pressure to avoid surface damage

Repairing damage

  • Small holes (under 1/2"): Fill with lightweight spackle, allow to dry, sand smooth
  • Medium holes (1/2"–3"): Use a mesh patch kit or self-adhesive patch, apply joint compound in thin coats, sand between coats
  • Large holes: Cut out the damaged section and install a backing board — then patch with drywall and joint compound
  • Cracks: Widen the crack slightly, fill with flexible caulk or joint compound depending on location
  • Woodwork gaps: Always caulk the joint between trim and wall before painting — this creates the clean, professional line clients expect

Sanding

  • Lightly sand any glossy surfaces before painting — paint slides off gloss without this step
  • Sand all patches smooth — run your hand over them. If you can feel it, you'll see it once painted.
  • Use a sanding sponge or hand block for walls; a pole sander for large flat areas
  • Always wipe sanding dust with a tack cloth or damp rag before painting
GritUse
60–80 gritRemoving old paint, rough wood, heavy material removal
100–120 gritGeneral surface prep, smoothing patches
150–180 gritFinal prep before painting, light scuffing of gloss
220 gritBetween coats for ultra-smooth finish, cabinets and trim

Priming — the step beginners skip

Primer is not optional. It is the foundation that determines whether your paint job lasts 2 years or 10.

  • New drywall: Always prime — raw drywall absorbs paint unevenly (called "flashing")
  • Repairs and patches: Always prime — patches absorb differently than surrounding wall
  • Color changes (dark to light): Use a tinted primer to reduce coats needed
  • Stains (water, smoke, nicotine): Use a stain-blocking primer (Zinsser BIN or Kilz) — regular primer will not block these
  • Bare wood: Always prime exterior wood before painting
⚠️ Skipping primer on stains is the most common beginner mistake. Apply two coats of regular paint over a water stain and it will bleed back through both coats. Use Zinsser BIN (shellac-based) and it disappears permanently.

Chapter 6 · Paint & Materials

Choosing the right product for every job.

Walk into any paint store and you'll face hundreds of products. Knowing how to choose quickly and correctly is a skill that separates professional painters from amateurs — and it's something employers will test you on immediately.

Latex vs. Oil-Based paint

PropertyLatex (Water-Based)Oil-Based
CleanupWater and soapMineral spirits / solvents
Dry time1–2 hours between coats8–24 hours between coats
DurabilityGood — improves with acrylicExcellent — very hard finish
FumesLow VOC, indoor-friendlyHigh VOC, strong ventilation required
Best useWalls, ceilings, most interior workTrim, cabinets, doors, high-wear surfaces
TrendIndustry standard for most workBeing replaced by waterborne alkyd in many applications

Paint finishes — know when to use each

⬜
Flat / Matte
Hides imperfections best. Low light reflection. Use on ceilings and low-traffic walls. Shows scuffs — not washable.
🥚
Eggshell
Slight sheen. More durable than flat. The standard finish for most interior walls. Easy to wipe clean.
✨
Satin
Smooth, moderate sheen. More durable and washable. Good for hallways, kitchens, kids' rooms.
💡
Semi-Gloss
Noticeably shiny. Highly durable and washable. Standard for trim, doors, and bathrooms.
🪞
Gloss
High shine. Hardest finish. Best for furniture, cabinets, and exterior trim that takes the most punishment.
ℹ️ Quick rule: Higher sheen = more durable and washable, but shows surface imperfections more. Lower sheen = hides imperfections better, but marks more easily. Always match the finish to the use case.

Interior vs. Exterior paint — never swap them

  • Exterior paints contain mildewcides, UV inhibitors, and flexibility agents to handle weather changes. Used indoors, they off-gas harmful chemicals.
  • Interior paints are formulated for indoor air quality. Used outdoors, they will peel, fade, and fail quickly.
  • This is not a cost-saving opportunity. Using the wrong product creates costly callbacks and damages your reputation.

Specialty products you'll encounter

  • Stain-blocking primer (Zinsser BIN, Kilz): Blocks water stains, smoke, and nicotine permanently
  • Masonry paint: Breathable, flexible. Required for concrete blocks, brick, and stucco
  • Epoxy floor coating: For garage floors and industrial surfaces — two-part system, very durable
  • Elastomeric paint: Bridges hairline cracks in stucco and masonry — flexible when dry
  • Waterborne alkyd: Oil-like finish with latex cleanup. Increasingly standard for trim and cabinets

Chapter 7 · Interior Painting

The technique that makes jobs look professional.

Interior painting is where most painters spend the majority of their time. Done right, it looks effortless. Done wrong, every flaw is visible every time the light hits the wall. Here is exactly how the pros do it.

The professional sequence — always follow this order

  1. Protect floors, furniture, and fixtures (drop cloths, masking, plastic)
  2. Remove outlet and switch plate covers
  3. Complete all surface prep (patch, sand, caulk, prime)
  4. Paint the ceiling first
  5. Paint the walls (cut in first, then roll)
  6. Paint the trim and doors last
💡 Always paint top to bottom and trim last. If you drip on the wall from the ceiling, you'll paint over it when you do the walls. If you drip on the trim from the walls, you'll paint over it when you do the trim.

Cutting in — the skill that defines your quality

Cutting in means painting a clean 2–3 inch border with a brush along all edges, corners, and trim before rolling. This is the single most visible skill a painter has. Clean cuts = professional work. Sloppy cuts = constant touch-ups and unhappy clients.

  • Load your angled brush — don't overload, about 1/3 of the bristles into the paint
  • Start about 1/2" from the edge and work toward it, letting the bristle tip do the work
  • Use a steady, smooth stroke — don't rush
  • Keep a wet edge — if your cut line dries before you roll, you'll see a line
  • Practice on cardboard before your first job — even experienced painters warm up

Rolling technique

  • Load roller fully — roll out excess on the tray grid until the roller is evenly saturated
  • Apply with a "W" or "M" pattern, then fill in without lifting the roller
  • Maintain consistent pressure — don't press harder in some areas than others
  • Keep a wet edge — always roll back into the section you just painted before it dries
  • Work in sections of about 4' x 4'
  • Finish with light vertical strokes from top to bottom to eliminate roller marks

Painting trim and woodwork

  • Paint with the grain — always stroke in the direction the wood runs
  • Use long, smooth strokes — avoid short, choppy strokes that create lap marks
  • Don't overwork the paint — once it begins to set, leave it alone. Brushing into drying paint creates drag marks.
  • For doors: paint panels first, then rails (horizontal pieces), then stiles (vertical pieces), then edges last
  • For windows: paint muntins (dividers) first, then frames, then sills

Chapter 8 · Exterior Painting

The job that weather controls.

Exterior painting follows the same principles as interior — prep, prime, paint — but weather, surface variety, and scale make it its own discipline. The pro who understands exterior work will never run out of jobs.

When to paint — and when to stop

  • Ideal temperature: 50°F–90°F. Below 50°F, paint won't cure properly. Above 90°F, it dries too fast and causes lap marks.
  • Never paint in direct sunlight — the surface temperature is often 20–30° hotter than air temperature
  • Don't paint if rain is expected within 24 hours
  • Humidity above 85% slows drying dramatically and can cause paint failure
  • Paint on the shaded side of a building, following the shade as the day progresses
⚠️ Painting over wet or damp wood is the most common cause of exterior paint failure. After rain, wait at least 24–48 hours. Test with a moisture meter — surface should read below 15% moisture content.

Exterior preparation — the full sequence

  1. Inspect: Check for rot, damaged siding, failed caulk, and deteriorated paint. Fix structural issues before painting.
  2. Clean: Pressure wash to remove dirt, mildew, and loose paint. Use a mildicide solution if mold is present.
  3. Scrape: Remove all loose and flaking paint — if it's not bonded, paint over it will fail too.
  4. Sand: Feather edges where old paint meets bare wood — visible ridges will show through the new coat.
  5. Caulk: Seal all gaps around windows, doors, trim joints, and siding seams. Use paintable exterior caulk.
  6. Prime: Prime all bare wood, repairs, and any areas where old paint was removed down to the substrate.

Exterior painting technique

  • Work from top to bottom — paint fascia and soffits first, then siding, then trim
  • On siding, paint the underside of the lap first, then the face
  • Use a brush for all trim and detail work; roller or sprayer for large flat siding areas
  • Maintain a wet edge — on large surfaces, work in manageable vertical sections
  • Back-brush sprayed surfaces — run a brush over freshly sprayed areas to work paint into the wood grain

Painting masonry (stucco, brick, concrete block)

  • Use masonry-specific paint — standard latex will not bond properly to most masonry
  • Fill cracks with masonry patching compound before painting
  • Remove efflorescence (white powder deposits) with a masonry cleaner before painting — paint will not bond over it
  • Apply two coats — masonry is highly porous and will absorb the first coat
  • Use a thick-nap roller (3/4"–1") to work paint into the texture

Chapter 9 · Specialty Finishes

Where you charge more.

Standard painting pays well. Specialty finishes pay better. These techniques set you apart from the $20/hour helper who only knows how to roll walls — and open the door to higher-end clients and bigger margins.

You don't need these on day one. Learn them after you're employed and comfortable with the basics. But know they exist and start watching pros do them.

Spray painting — the production tool

  • Hold the sprayer 10–12" from the surface at all times
  • Move in smooth, overlapping strokes — overlap each pass by 50%
  • Keep the gun perpendicular to the surface — tilting creates uneven coverage
  • Trigger before reaching the surface, release after passing it — prevents heavy spots at each end
  • Multiple thin coats always beat one heavy coat — drips are almost impossible to fix cleanly
  • Mask everything nearby — overspray travels further than you think

Cabinet and furniture painting

Cabinets are high-value work and clients pay premium prices for quality results. Mistakes are visible every day.

  • Remove all doors and hardware — never paint cabinets in place
  • Clean thoroughly — kitchen grease prevents adhesion; use degreaser
  • Sand all surfaces to 150 grit, then 220 grit
  • Apply a bonding primer — Stix or BIN are the professional standards
  • Spray with an HVLP for the smoothest finish — no brush or roller marks
  • 2–3 thin coats of waterborne alkyd enamel for maximum durability
  • Sand lightly between coats with 320 grit

Decorative and faux finishes

  • Color washing: A diluted glaze applied over base coat and manipulated with a brush creates a soft, layered depth
  • Sponging: Use a natural sea sponge to apply or remove glaze for organic texture
  • Ragging: Crumpled rag creates patterns in wet glaze — each rag creates a unique texture
  • Stippling: Brush is dabbed into wet glaze to create fine texture — classic for ceilings
  • Stenciling: Secure stencil firmly, use a nearly dry brush in dabbing motions — never stroke the brush or paint bleeds under the edges

Texture coatings

  • Knockdown texture — joint compound applied by roller or sprayer, then partially smoothed with a knife
  • Orange peel texture — thin compound sprayed to create a bumpy texture resembling citrus skin
  • Skip trowel — compound applied by hand with a trowel in irregular overlapping passes
  • Smooth texture removal — skim coating over textured walls; high-value skill because almost no painters do it well

Chapter 10 · Estimating & Business Skills

The math that makes you profitable.

Most painters who work for themselves fail not because they can't paint — but because they can't estimate. They underbid jobs, run out of money, and burn out. Learning to estimate properly is what separates a painter with a job from a painter with a business.

You don't need this on day one. But learn it while you're employed — because the sooner you understand the numbers, the sooner you can control them.

Measuring square footage

  • Wall area: Measure width × height of each wall, add them together, subtract doors (20 sq ft each) and windows (15 sq ft each)
  • Ceiling area: Length × width of the room
  • Trim: Measure linear feet of baseboard, door casings, crown molding, and window trim
  • Rule of thumb: A gallon of latex paint covers 350–400 sq ft per coat on a smooth surface

Calculating materials

Interior latex paint350–400 sq ft/gallonReduce by 20% on textured surfaces

Estimating labor — the part most beginners get wrong

  • Prep takes as long as — often longer than — the actual painting
  • A 12' × 15' room (one coat) takes an experienced painter roughly 2–3 hours from setup to cleanup
  • Always add time for: protecting floors and furniture, moving items, cutting in, two coats, and cleanup
  • When starting out, track your time on every job — your estimates will get more accurate with each project

The path from employee to contractor

When you're ready to work for yourself, here's what it takes:

  • Contractor's license: Most states require one for jobs over a certain dollar amount — check your state's licensing board
  • Business license: Register your business (LLC recommended for liability protection) — typically $50–$200
  • Insurance: General liability ($1M minimum) is required by most clients. About $100–$200/month for a solo operator.
  • Estimating software: Quick Bid, Jobber, or simply a well-designed spreadsheet
  • Marketing: Google Business Profile (free), Nextdoor (free), and before/after photos on Instagram are how most small painting businesses get their first clients
💡 The fastest path to running your own painting business: work for a good contractor for 2–3 years, learn every aspect of the trade and how they run their business, save money, and take your first clients on weekends. Never leave your job until your side business replaces your income.

Chapter 11 · Getting Hired

Everything you've learned — now put it to work.

If you've worked through this course, you already know more than most people who apply for helper positions. Now let's make sure you get the job.

What employers are actually looking for

Here's the honest truth about hiring at the helper level: most painting contractors are not looking for someone who knows everything. They're looking for someone who is safe, reliable, and teachable. Technical knowledge is a bonus at this stage. Character is the requirement.

Must Have
The first thing most contractors check. Get it before you apply to anything.
🚗
Reliable Transportation
Must Have
Job sites change. A valid license and reliable vehicle are frequently required.
🎨
Trade Knowledge
Strong Advantage
Being able to explain prep steps, paint types, and tool names immediately sets you apart.
📜
PCA Certificate
Strong Advantage
Employers notice the person who started learning before they got hired.
💪
Physical Readiness
Big Plus
Standing, climbing, lifting, and repetitive motion all day. Mention your physical fitness. Employers appreciate it.
🔥
Genuine Enthusiasm
Most Important
Contractors hire people they want to spend 8 hours a day with. Someone excited about the trade is worth more than any certificate.

Where to find painting jobs

  • Indeed.com ↗ — search "painter helper" or "painting laborer no experience"
  • ZipRecruiter.com ↗ — "painter helper no experience"
  • Craigslist — Skilled Trades — many small painting contractors post here exclusively, check daily
  • Google Maps — search "painting contractor [your city]" and call every one directly
  • Nextdoor and Facebook Marketplace — small residential painters often post jobs here
  • Paint supply stores — Sherwin-Williams, Benjamin Moore, PPG stores. Contractors come in every morning. Introduce yourself.

Words that work in painting interviews

💬 Be honest: "I have no field experience yet, but I've completed this course, earned my OSHA 10 card, and I'm serious about building a career in this trade. I'm a hard worker, I show up early, and I'll do whatever prep work the job needs without complaining."
  • Be ready to explain the correct order of a paint job (prep → prime → paint → trim last)
  • Name a few paint finishes and when you'd use each one
  • Explain why surface prep matters more than the paint itself
  • Emphasize attitude: "I show up on time, I'm physically fit, I follow direction, and I ask questions when I'm unsure."
  • Ask smart questions: "What types of projects does your company mostly work on?" — shows you understand the trade has different sectors
🎯 Target small to mid-size residential painting contractors. A company with 3–15 painters will give you real responsibility much faster than a large commercial firm. They train as they go and are far more likely to hire someone with no field experience.

Once you're hired — remember this

You now have the knowledge and know-how to get in the door. What happens next is entirely up to you. Practice, practice, practice. Every wall you prep, every line you cut, every surface you roll — that's where real skill is built. No course gives you that. Only the job does.

The painters who advance fastest are not the most naturally talented. They're the ones who show up every single day, pay attention to everything around them, and treat every job as a classroom. That's you now. Go get started.

The next level — building your own business

Once you're proficient — once the prep is second nature, the cut lines are clean, and you're trusted on any job — the painting trade offers something most careers don't: a clear, affordable path to full business ownership.

Tim Oliphant, whose video is in Chapter 1, started with a few hundred dollars and built a six-figure solo business. He's not unusual. The painting industry has one of the lowest barriers to entrepreneurship of any skilled trade — no expensive equipment, no massive overhead, no years of licensing hurdles before you can work for yourself.
